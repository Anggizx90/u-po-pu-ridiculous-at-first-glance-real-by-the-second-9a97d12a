# Candidate pitches — Ridiculous at First Glance, Real by the Second

The judge's criteria, in the order the judge weighs them:
1. The sentence pitches a bizarre startup idea that sounds ridiculous at first
   glance yet makes sense as a real business.
2. Genuinely bizarre — not a familiar business with a strange adjective.
3. Business logic holds — the sentence makes clear why someone would pay.
4. Lands as a pitch — one sentence, ≤200 chars, a turn from laughing to nodding.
5. Concrete and vivid — names the thing sold and who buys it.

Scores below are B (bizarre/novel), L (logic/payer), P (pitch turn),
C (concrete) out of 5 each, judged in that priority order.

## The pool (14 candidates)

1. **Plant funerals** — "We run funerals for houseplants: $29 buys owners who
   named their fern a tiny coffin, a eulogy and a dignified composting, because
   nobody throws a family member in the kitchen bin."
   B4 L5 P4 C5 = 18. Pet death-care is a proven multi-billion market; the plant
   version is the novel extension. Price, product bundle and payer all named;
   the turn is the emotional fact that a named plant is family.
2. **Ghost-free certification** — "Sellers pay us to haunt their unsellable
   house for one weekend, then certify it ghost-free since Tuesday, because a
   tested haunting scares buyers less than a rumoured one."
   B5 L4 P5 C4 = 18. Strangest premise and the sharpest turn, but the money
   step (paying someone to haunt your own house first) is one beat odder than
   the payoff, so its logic trails #1.
3. **Disappointed-mother voicemails** — "Procrastinators pay us $12 a month for
   a daily voicemail from a stranger reading their to-do list in a disappointed
   mother's voice, because guilt finishes more tasks than any app."
   B4 L4 P5 C4 = 17. Great turn; the payer is real but the product is a voice
   gimmick on top of the familiar accountability-app market.
4. **Raven resignation delivery** — "For $40, our trained raven delivers your
   resignation to your boss's desk, because nobody negotiates with a bird that
   has already dropped 'I quit' on the keyboard."
   B5 L3 P4 C5 = 17. Never-heard-of premise, very concrete, but the reason to
   pay $40 instead of emailing is spectacle alone — thinner money logic.
5. **New-car smell bottling** — "We bottle new-car smell and sell it to
   used-car dealers, who spray one puff into a 2014 hatchback and add $300 to
   the price, because noses sign more finance deals than engines do."
   B3 L5 P4 C5 = 17. Airtight dealer economics, but scent sprays for cars
   already exist, so criterion 2 (never heard of it) is weaker.
6. **Streamer studio audience** — "Lonely streamers rent our live studio
   audience of eight, because chat speeds up, tips triple and sponsors call
   back when somebody is already laughing at the jokes."
   B4 L5 P4 C4 = 17. Real social-proof economics and a clear payer; adjacent
   to rent-a-crowd, which costs it a point of novelty.
7. **Fashion scarecrows** — "Farmers pay us to redress their scarecrows in this
   season's fast fashion, because birds ignore a static figure but flee a
   mannequin whose outfit changed overnight."
   B4 L5 P3 C5 = 17. The logic is genuinely real (birds habituate to a static
   figure), but the sentence explains more than it turns.
8. **Dog love letters** — "For $19 a month your dog receives love letters from
   a secret admirer, because owners convinced their pet has a romance buy every
   treat, toy and portrait we tuck inside the envelope."
   B4 L4 P4 C5 = 17. Vivid and commercial; the payer's motive is one inference
   longer than #1's.
9. **Rent-a-grandmother for pitch meetings** — "Startups rent our grandmothers
   by the hour to sit in pitch meetings nodding, because investors fund faster
   when someone's nan asks where the profits are and means it."
   B3 L4 P4 C4 = 15. Rent-a-family services already exist in Japan, so the
   premise is less unheard-of than it first looks.
10. **Group-chat funerals** — "We hold funerals for dead group chats: for $6 a
    moderator reads the last memes aloud, archives the photos and formally
    dissolves the chat, because 40 adults cannot let 'Ibiza 2019' die in
    silence."
    B5 L3 P4 C4 = 16. Very fresh premise; whether 40 adults actually pay is
    the weakest money story in the top half.
11. **Monstera watering for exes** — "We water the houseplants of people who
    moved out after a breakup, charging $15 a visit, because the monstera is
    the one thing both exes still agree must not die."
    B3 L4 P4 C5 = 16. Concrete and human, but plant-sitting is a familiar
    service wearing a breakup costume.
12. **Wedding-speech insurance** — "For $99 we insure your best man's speech:
    if the toast bombs, our actor stands, claims to be the groom's childhood
    self, and tells the story that saves the room."
    B4 L3 P4 C4 = 15. Funny, but the buyer has to purchase before knowing the
    speech bombs, which tangles the logic.
13. **Professional hotel guests** — "Hotels pay our professional guests to
    sleep in unsold rooms and praise the breakfast, because an empty hotel
    photographs like a failing one and full-looking ones book out."
    B3 L4 P3 C4 = 14. Rent-a-crowd in a hotel costume; the turn is soft.
14. **Wake-up arguers** — "Our humans phone you at 5am and stay on the line
    arguing until you are vertical, charging $2 a call, because a snooze button
    cannot call you a coward."
    B3 L4 P4 C3 = 14. Human wake-up calls have existed as apps; the payer and
    product blur together.

## Head-to-head among the finalists

#1 vs #2 (the two 18s): criterion 2 differs slightly for #2 (ghost-testing is
the stranger premise); criterion 3 differs more for #1 — a priced bundle sold
into a proven adjacent market (pet death-care) against a certification whose
first step the buyer must be talked into. The judge's own rule: a later
criterion that differs by much more decides. #1 wins on logic by more than it
loses on novelty.

#1 vs #4: #4 is stranger, but its $40 buys spectacle, not a solved problem;
#1's $29 buys guilt relief a known market already pays for at pet scale.

#1 vs #5/#6/#7 (the 17s): each is sound, but each is either a known product
(car scent), a known service shape (rent-a-crowd, scarecrow redressing is an
explanation, dog mail is a marketing channel) — #1's premise, a funeral —
coffin, eulogy, composting — for a houseplant is the one a reader has not
heard, and its payer's reason is the most immediate.

## Winner

"We run funerals for houseplants: $29 buys owners who named their fern a tiny
coffin, a eulogy and a dignified composting, because nobody throws a family
member in the kitchen bin."

One sentence; names the thing sold (a houseplant funeral: coffin, eulogy,
composting), who pays (owners who named their fern) and the price ($29), and
turns on the nod: a named plant is family, and family does not go in the bin.
